1 · Wix setup, in order

  1. Create the account in Jessica's name. Use her email so she owns the site.
  2. Start from a blank template (“Start from scratch” in the Wix Editor). Templates fight this design.
  3. Decide the main language first. It's hard to change later. The audience is mainly Spanish speakers, so Spanish as the main language is worth considering. Then add the other language with Wix Multilingual (Settings → Multilingual).
  4. Set the colors and fonts in Site Styles before building any page (section 2).
  5. Add the apps: Wix Blog (articles), Wix Bookings or a button to the Google Calendar link (consultations), Wix Forms + Wix Email Marketing (newsletter), Wix Site Search.
  6. Build the header and footer once, then the pages in this order: Home → Articles → Symptoms → Supplements → About → Resources → the small pages.
  7. Load the content from sections 6–11 of this guide.
  8. Check every page on mobile (the phone icon in the editor). Phone layout is edited separately in Wix.
  9. Buy a Premium plan (needed to use her own domain and remove Wix ads), then connect ellabienestar.com: Settings → Domains → Connect a domain you already own. Wix walks you through the Namecheap steps.
  10. Re-check email after connecting the domain. If info@ellabienestar.com stops receiving mail, its email (MX) records need to be re-added in Wix's DNS settings.

1. Menopause and perimenopause: what’s the difference?

Menopause and perimenopause: what’s the difference?

These two words get used interchangeably, and they shouldn’t be. Knowing which one you’re in changes what you do about it.

Key takeaways
  • Menopause is 12 consecutive months without a period. Everything before that is perimenopause.
  • Perimenopause usually happens naturally between 45 and 56, but surgery or chemotherapy can bring it on earlier.
  • Symptoms live in perimenopause, because that is where hormones fluctuate.
  • Perimenopause is also the window where hormone therapy is safest and most effective to start.

The purpose of this website is to make this journey as easy as possible for you. I have done the research, and I will keep going as new developments come out. If I can help even one person spend less energy on this and more on the rest of her life, it is worth it.

Menopause

Menopause is the point when menstruation has stopped for at least 12 consecutive months, because the ovaries are no longer producing estrogen, progesterone, and other hormones. It is a single marker in time, looking backward.

Perimenopause

Perimenopause is everything before that. It runs from the onset of significant fluctuations in ovarian function to one year after your final menstrual period. This usually happens naturally between the ages of 45 and 56, but it can also be brought on by surgery or by treatments for other conditions, like chemotherapy.

“Most women are in perimenopause far longer than they realize, and that is the stretch where the symptoms live.”

Why the distinction matters

Perimenopause is where the fluctuation happens, and fluctuation is what causes symptoms. It is also the window where hormone therapy is most effective and safest to start. If you are having symptoms in your forties and your periods are still coming, you are almost certainly perimenopausal, and that is useful information to walk into an appointment with.

2. Inflammation: the thing I wish someone had explained first.

Inflammation: the thing I wish someone had explained first.

Almost everything that changes in menopause traces back to one mechanism. Understanding it made every other decision easier for me.

Key takeaways
  • Inflammation is a normal defense system. It evolved to protect us from infection and injury.
  • One of estrogen’s jobs is reducing inflammation, so when estrogen drops, that brake comes off.
  • Unchecked inflammation connects heart disease, brain fog, joint pain, GI issues, mood changes, and weight changes.
  • Reducing inflammation is the single lens worth organizing your decisions around.

Before anything else, I have to talk about inflammation. It is the thread that connects nearly every symptom, and once I understood it, everything else I did made more sense.

Why we evolved to be inflammatory

Inflammation is an essential part of our defense system. It evolved to protect us from infection, injury, and toxins. Without it, we would quickly succumb to infections and that would be the end of it. A fever is a good example: it is an inflammatory response, and it helps us fight infection. So inflammation exists for a good reason.

Where estrogen comes in

Here is the part that surprised me most. One of estrogen’s roles is reducing inflammation. So imagine what happens when estrogen drops, or stops altogether. The brake comes off.

“One of estrogen’s jobs is to keep inflammation in check. When estrogen leaves, inflammation stops being managed.”

What unchecked inflammation looks like

  • Heart disease and cardiovascular risk
  • Memory fog and difficulty concentrating
  • Joint pain and morning stiffness
  • Digestive and GI problems
  • Mental health changes, anxiety and low mood
  • Weight changes that do not respond the way they used to

From day one, my whole approach has been organized around a single question: how do I reduce as much inflammation as possible? Diet, movement, supplements, sleep, and hormone therapy all sit underneath that question. That is the lens I use for everything on this site.

3. Estrogen: what it actually does.

EN

Menopause · 5 min read · August 2026

Estrogen: what it actually does.

Most of us learned estrogen was about reproduction. It is doing far more than that, which is why losing it affects so much.

Key takeaways
  • Estrogen is not only about reproduction. It supports bone, heart, and brain health, plus mood regulation.
  • It also has an anti-inflammatory role, which is why losing it affects so many systems at once.
  • In perimenopause levels fluctuate; in menopause the ovaries stop producing it.
  • This is why symptoms feel scattered and unrelated. They are related.

Estrogen is a hormone produced in the ovaries, primarily known for its role in regulating the female reproductive system. That is the version most of us were taught, and it is incomplete.

The jobs nobody mentions

Estrogen is key for bone health, heart health, and brain health, as well as mood regulation. And, as covered elsewhere on this site, it reduces inflammation.

So what happens when it declines

During perimenopause, levels start decreasing and fluctuating. In menopause, the ovaries stop producing estrogen altogether. All of those important functions, the ones above, become chaotic. Inflammation takes over.

“It is not that one system breaks. It is that the hormone quietly supporting several systems at once is no longer there.”

This is why menopause symptoms feel so scattered and so hard to explain to a doctor in a ten-minute visit. Joint pain, brain fog, a racing heart, and low mood do not sound related. They are.

4. Your bones after estrogen.

EN

Exercise · 6 min read · August 2026

Your bones after estrogen.

Bone loss accelerates sharply after menopause. Here is the mechanism, and the two things that reliably help.

Key takeaways
  • Estrogen keeps bone remodeling in balance. When it drops, the brake on bone breakdown comes off.
  • Accelerated bone loss is osteoporosis, and it means higher fracture risk.
  • Calcium, vitamin D, and vitamin K2 all play distinct roles in bone health.
  • Combine weight-bearing, resistance, balance, and impact work. Consistency beats intensity.

Bone is not static. It is constantly being broken down and rebuilt, in a finely tuned balance. Estrogen is part of what keeps that balance steady.

What happens when estrogen declines

When circulating estrogen declines significantly, that balance is severely disrupted. This most commonly happens after menopause, when ovarian function ceases and estrogen production drops sharply. The lack of estrogen removes the brake on osteoclast activity, the cells that break bone down, leading to accelerated bone loss. That condition is called osteoporosis, and it means a higher risk of fractures and breaks.

Supplements that support bone

Calcium and vitamin D are crucial for bone health in menopausal and postmenopausal women, for exactly the reason above. Calcium aids in building and maintaining strong bones, while vitamin D improves calcium absorption. Vitamin K2 also plays a role in bone metabolism, helping direct calcium into bone rather than into arteries.

Exercise that supports bone

A balanced program combining weight-bearing, resistance, balance, and impact activities is most effective for maintaining and improving bone health during and after menopause. Consistency, gradual progression, and attention to safety are what reduce fracture risk over time.

“Consistency matters more than intensity. Bone responds to being asked to do something, regularly.”
  • Weight-bearing: walking, hiking, stair climbing, dancing
  • Resistance: bands, dumbbells, bodyweight movements
  • Balance: single-leg work, tai chi, yoga
  • Impact: light hopping or jumping, if your joints allow it

Tus huesos cuando baja el estrógeno.

Después de la menopausia los huesos se debilitan mucho más rápido. Aquí te explico por qué, y las dos cosas que sí ayudan.

Lo esencial
  • El estrógeno mantiene en equilibrio la forma en que el hueso se renueva. Cuando baja, el hueso se desgasta más rápido de lo que se reconstruye.
  • Cuando el hueso se pierde así de rápido se llama osteoporosis, y aumenta el riesgo de fracturas.
  • El calcio, la vitamina D y la vitamina K2 cumplen funciones distintas en la salud de los huesos.
  • Combina ejercicios con peso, de fuerza, de equilibrio y de impacto. Ser constante importa más que la intensidad.

El hueso no se queda igual. Todo el tiempo se desgasta y se vuelve a formar, en un equilibrio muy delicado. El estrógeno es parte de lo que mantiene ese equilibrio.

Qué pasa cuando baja el estrógeno

Cuando el estrógeno baja mucho, ese equilibrio se rompe. Casi siempre pasa después de la menopausia, cuando los ovarios dejan de funcionar y el estrógeno cae de golpe. Sin estrógeno, los osteoclastos, las células que desgastan el hueso, trabajan sin freno, y el hueso se pierde más rápido. Eso se llama osteoporosis, y aumenta el riesgo de fracturas.

Suplementos para los huesos

El calcio y la vitamina D son muy importantes para los huesos durante y después de la menopausia, justo por lo que expliqué arriba. El calcio ayuda a formar y mantener huesos fuertes, y la vitamina D ayuda a que el cuerpo absorba el calcio. La vitamina K2 también ayuda, porque manda el calcio a los huesos en vez de a las arterias.

Ejercicio para los huesos

Lo que mejor funciona para cuidar y fortalecer los huesos durante y después de la menopausia es una rutina que combine ejercicios con peso, de fuerza, de equilibrio y de impacto. Ser constante, ir subiendo poco a poco y cuidarte de no lastimarte es lo que baja el riesgo de fracturas con el tiempo.

«Ser constante importa más que la intensidad. El hueso responde cuando le pides que trabaje, una y otra vez.»
  • Carga: caminar, senderismo, subir escaleras, bailar
  • Fuerza: ligas, mancuernas, ejercicios con tu propio peso
  • Equilibrio: ejercicios en una pierna, tai chi, yoga
  • Impacto: saltos ligeros, si tus articulaciones lo permiten

5. Heart health after menopause.

EN

Menopause · 6 min read · August 2026

Heart health after menopause.

Cardiovascular risk rises meaningfully after menopause, because you have lost years of estrogen’s protective effect. This is the most actionable part.

Key takeaways
  • Cardiovascular risk rises after menopause because you lose 5 to 10 years of estrogen’s protective effect.
  • A Mediterranean-style eating pattern is the best-supported dietary approach.
  • Target 150 minutes of vigorous exercise weekly, with strength training on alternate days.
  • Strength training is about combating muscle loss, not bodybuilding. It also protects bone.
  • 10,000 steps daily has been shown to reduce belly fat.

Menopause increases your risk of cardiovascular events, like a heart attack, significantly. The reason is straightforward: you have lost five to ten years of the protective effect of estrogen. Estrogen protects our heart and our blood vessels, and once it is gone, that protection goes with it.

Diet

A heart-healthy diet, such as the Mediterranean diet, is the most well-supported dietary pattern here. It is not a restrictive plan, it is a shift toward olive oil, fish, legumes, vegetables, and whole grains.

Supplements

Omega-3 reduces inflammation and supports heart health. Vitamin D also plays a role. Both are covered in more detail in the supplements section.

Exercise

The recommendation is 150 minutes of vigorous exercise each week. That should include moderate to high intensity aerobic activity at least three times a week, combined with strength training on other days.

“Strength training is not about powerlifting or bodybuilding. It is about combating muscle loss so you can stay active and keep doing everyday things as you age.”

It has also been shown that muscle strength protects our skeletal system and our bones, so strength work is doing double duty.

Walking

At least 10,000 steps daily has been shown to reduce belly fat. It is the most accessible intervention on this page, and it requires nothing but time.

6. HRT and natural alternatives.

EN

Menopause · 7 min read · August 2026

HRT and natural alternatives.

Hormone therapy is the most effective and best-studied treatment for menopause symptoms. Here are the forms, the timing that matters, and one critical safety point.

Key takeaways
  • HRT is the most effective and best-studied treatment for perimenopause and menopause symptoms.
  • It is safest and most effective started during perimenopause or within 10 years of menopause onset.
  • Critical: if you still have a uterus and take estrogen, you must also take progesterone to protect against uterine cancer.
  • Phytoestrogens (soy, flax, legumes) and adaptogens (Rhodiola, Ashwagandha) are alternatives worth discussing with your doctor.

Hormone replacement therapy, or HRT, is the most effective and well-studied treatment for perimenopause and menopause symptoms. It comes in multiple delivery options and has a strong safety profile when tailored to your individual health needs.

Timing matters

HRT is most effective and safest when started during perimenopause, or within ten years of menopause onset. This window is one of the most important things to know, because it is often the difference between HRT being a straightforward option and a more complicated conversation.

Forms of estrogen

  • Pills
  • Creams and other topicals
  • Patches

The critical safety point

“If you still have a uterus and you are taking estrogen, it is critical to also take progesterone. This protects you from developing uterine cancer.”

This is not optional and it is not a detail. If a provider offers you estrogen alone and you still have a uterus, ask about progesterone.

Forms of progesterone

  • Pills
  • Creams and other topicals
  • An IUD that contains progesterone

Natural alternatives

Natural alternatives to hormone replacement therapy include phytoestrogens and adaptogens, which offer symptom relief through distinct but complementary mechanisms.

Phytoestrogens, found in soy, flax seeds, and legumes, modulate estrogen receptors and may reduce hot flashes and bone loss. Adaptogens like Rhodiola and Ashwagandha improve stress resilience and hormonal balance.

These options support holistic, individualized care, with an emphasis on safety and monitoring. They are worth discussing with your doctor rather than starting alone.

Fatty fish, egg yolks, fortified milk. Food rarely gets you there on its own. Sunlight is the main source, and it drops off in winter.

What to look for on the label

D3 (cholecalciferol), not D2. Take it with a meal that contains fat, and pair it with K2.

How much

Get a blood test first. Many providers suggest 1000–2000 IU/day depending on your result.

Be careful if

Fat-soluble, so it builds up in the body. This is the one to test before and after rather than guess at.

4. Vitamin K2

EN

BONES

Vitamin K2

Pairs with Vit D to get calcium where it belongs.

Why it matters now

  • Vitamin K helps direct calcium into your bones and keep it out of your arteries.
  • It works hand-in-hand with vitamin D, taking them together makes more sense than either alone.

Get it from food first

Natto, aged cheeses, egg yolks. Dark leafy greens give you K1, which is related but not the same thing.

What to look for on the label

MK-7 stays active in the body far longer than MK-4. It is often already included in a good D3 supplement, so check before buying both.

How much

Often combined with vitamin D in a single supplement (K2/MK-7 form).

Be careful if

If you take warfarin, do not add K2 without talking to your doctor. Vitamin K directly affects how that medication works.

The evidence

Evidence-based fact sheet from the NIH. NIH Office of Dietary Supplements · https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/VitaminK-Consumer/

5. Magnesium L-Threonate

EN

SLEEP · STRESS

Magnesium L-Threonate

Crosses into the brain better. Supports sleep, calm, and memory.

Why it matters now

  • Magnesium helps regulate the nervous system, muscle relaxation, and sleep, three things menopause tends to disrupt.
  • The L-threonate form crosses the blood-brain barrier better than other forms, so it’s more associated with cognitive and memory benefits.
  • It also plays a role in bone health and blood-sugar regulation.

Get it from food first

Pumpkin seeds, almonds, spinach, black beans, dark chocolate.

What to look for on the label

L-threonate is the one studied for brain and sleep. Glycinate is gentler and cheaper for general use. Skip magnesium oxide. It is poorly absorbed and mostly a laxative.

How much

Follow the product dosing, typically ~1–2 g a day of magnesium L-threonate.

Be careful if

Can loosen stools, so start at a low dose. Ask first if you have kidney disease.

The evidence

Evidence-based fact sheet from the NIH. NIH Office of Dietary Supplements · https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Magnesium-Consumer/

ES

SUEÑO · ESTRÉS

Magnesium L-Threonate

Llega mejor al cerebro. Ayuda con el sueño, la calma y la memoria.

Semillas de calabaza, almendras, espinacas, frijoles negros, chocolate amargo.

6. Turmeric

EN

INFLAMMATION · JOINTS

Turmeric

Natural anti-inflammatory. Helps with joint pain and swelling.

Why it matters now

  • Curcumin, turmeric’s active compound, has anti-inflammatory properties that may help with the joint pain common in perimenopause.
  • Some studies suggest added benefits for mood and cardiovascular health.

Get it from food first

Turmeric in cooking, always with black pepper and a little fat. That is what makes it absorbable.

What to look for on the label

A standardized curcumin extract with piperine, or a phospholipid form. Plain "turmeric powder" capsules are mostly filler.

How much

Look for curcumin with piperine or a bioavailable form, ~500–1000 mg a day.

Be careful if

Mildly blood-thinning at high doses and hard on an empty stomach. Avoid it if you have gallstones.

The evidence

Evidence is promising but still limited; talk to your doctor.

Turmeric

7. Fiber

EN

GUT · HORMONES

Fiber

Aim for 25 g a day. Helps estrogen leave the body the way it should.

Why it matters now

  • Fiber feeds the gut bacteria that help metabolize and clear estrogen in a balanced way.
  • It steadies blood sugar and fullness, helpful when metabolism shifts in menopause.
  • It supports heart health by helping manage cholesterol.

Get it from food first

Beans, lentils, oats, berries, chia, vegetables. This is the one where food genuinely beats a supplement.

What to look for on the label

If you do supplement, psyllium husk has the most evidence behind it. Take it with a full glass of water.

How much

Aim for ~25 g a day, ideally from food; a supplement can fill the gap.

Be careful if

Increase slowly or you will bloat. Take it a couple of hours apart from any medication so it does not block absorption.

The evidence

General public-health guidance.

ES

DIGESTIÓN · HORMONAS

Fiber

La meta es 25 g al día. Ayuda a que el cuerpo elimine bien el estrógeno.

Por qué importa ahora

  • La fibra alimenta a las bacterias del intestino que ayudan a procesar y eliminar el estrógeno de forma equilibrada.
  • Ayuda a controlar el azúcar en la sangre y a sentirte llena, algo útil cuando el metabolismo cambia en la menopausia.

Primero, la comida

Frijoles, lentejas, avena, moras, chía, verduras. Aquí la comida le gana al suplemento.

Qué buscar en la etiqueta

Si tomas suplemento, la cáscara de psyllium es la que tiene más evidencia. Tómala con un vaso grande de agua.

Cuánto

La meta es ~25 g al día, de preferencia de la comida; un suplemento puede completar lo que falte.

Ten cuidado si

Súbele poco a poco o te vas a inflamar. Tómala un par de horas antes o después de cualquier medicamento.

Recomendación general de salud pública.

FAQ

EN

Do I need supplements if I eat well?
Food is the base. But a few gaps are common at this stage even on a good diet: vitamin D in low-sunlight months, omega-3 if you don’t eat oily fish two or three times a week, magnesium under sustained stress, and fiber (most people get about half of what’s recommended). A blood test tells you more than guessing.

How do I tell a good supplement from a bad one?
Three checks. The form of the active ingredient: D3 not D2, magnesium glycinate or L-threonate rather than oxide, EPA and DHA listed by amount. The dose compared to what the studies actually used. And the excipient list, which should be short and recognizable. Serious brands publish third-party certificates of analysis.

How long before I notice anything?
It depends. Magnesium for sleep can show up within days. Vitamin D takes six to eight weeks to move your blood levels. Omega-3 for inflammation, eight to twelve. Consistency matters more than the size of the dose.

Can I take these with my medication?
Some interact. K2 with warfarin, omega-3 and turmeric with blood thinners, fiber with the timing of almost any pill. Bring the actual bottles to your next appointment and go through them with your doctor.

ES

¿Necesito suplementos si como bien?
La comida es la base. Pero a esta edad es común que falten algunas cosas aunque comas bien: vitamina D en los meses con poco sol, omega-3 si no comes pescado dos o tres veces a la semana, magnesio si vives con mucho estrés, y fibra (casi todas comemos la mitad de lo recomendado). Un análisis de sangre te dice más que adivinar.

¿Cómo sé si un suplemento es bueno?
Fíjate en tres cosas. La forma: D3 y no D2, magnesio glicinato o L-treonato y no óxido, y que diga cuánto EPA y DHA trae. La dosis, comparada con la que se usó en los estudios. Y los ingredientes extra: pocos y que los reconozcas. Las marcas serias publican análisis de laboratorios independientes.

¿En cuánto tiempo voy a notar algo?
Depende. El magnesio para dormir se puede notar en días. La vitamina D tarda de seis a ocho semanas en subir en la sangre. El omega-3 para la inflamación, de ocho a doce. Ser constante importa más que la dosis.

¿Los puedo tomar con mis medicamentos?
Algunos sí interactúan: la K2 con la warfarina, el omega-3 y la cúrcuma con los anticoagulantes, y la fibra con el horario de casi cualquier pastilla. Lleva los frascos a tu próxima cita y revísalos con tu médico.
